Chugoku, Kii Peninsula, Shikoku

In western Japan, small numbers are the point

These populations are small enough that Japan lists them as at risk of disappearing. That is a conservation figure. It is not a figure about your chance of meeting one.

Gear and behaviour do not guarantee that you will avoid an encounter or injury. Check the municipality and the trail or park manager for the mountain you choose.

Last updated: September 23, 2026

Populations listed as at risk

The Ministry of the Environment's red list treats the Shikoku, Chugoku, Kii Peninsula and Shimokita Peninsula groups as local populations at risk of extinction. The Shikoku population is put at somewhere between roughly ten and a few dozen animals.

Genetically, the Asian black bear splits into three groups: east of Lake Biwa, west of Lake Biwa, and the Kii Peninsula together with Shikoku. The western groups sit where they are not easily replenished from elsewhere.

Do not read "few" as "not me"

A small population is a sign that the animals are in trouble, not a sign that hikers are. A few dozen animals still means one of them can be on the trail you are walking. Do not carry less, or walk differently, because you are in western Japan.

Preparation and encounter behaviour are the same as for the Asian black bear elsewhere on Honshu. See black bear guidance, which follows Nagano Prefecture's advice.

Check each destination

Prefectures in western Japan differ in how they manage these populations and in how they publish sighting information. Look up the municipality for the mountain you plan to walk, and the manager of the trail itself. To start from the region level again, go back to Choose a hiking region.
